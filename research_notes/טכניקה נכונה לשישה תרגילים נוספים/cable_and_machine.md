# Cable and machine exercises: lat pulldown, triceps pushdown, 45-degree leg press (art-direction spec for a 5-second single-rep loop)

Access notes for the report writer: PubMed pages refused automated fetches (cookie wall), and the Kolber 2010 review PDF did not yield readable text, so the primary studies below are cited from their PubMed URL plus the abstract details reported in search results. That is marked where it applies. No ACE library page for the leg press was found, and none of NSCA, ACSM or ExRx was reached for these three exercises. Numbers marked **[illustration default]** are my geometric choices for the animator, not published values.

## Lat pulldown (cable, wide overhand grip, seated, thighs under pads)

### Takeaway
ACE gives the clearest spec. Thigh pad snug on the thighs. Shoulder blades set down and back first. A slight backward lean of no more than about 30 degrees, which does **not** increase during the pull. Bar to the upper or mid chest, elbows driving down toward the sides of the torso. Stop when the elbows stop moving down. Return slowly to full elbow extension, shoulder blades rising slightly at the top. Pull in front of the neck, never behind it. The behind-neck version has no lat advantage and puts the shoulder in the abducted, externally rotated "high-five" position that is linked to shoulder pain.

### Cited Findings
- Setup: sit and adjust the thigh pad so it sits firmly on top of the thighs to anchor the lower body. Brace the abdominals and keep the low back from arching. Grip the bar firmly with both hands. The ACE page does not specify grip width or orientation. — [ACE Lat Pulldown](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/158/lat-pulldown/)
- With the arms extended overhead, depress and retract the shoulder blades (down and back) and hold that through the set. Start the movement "by lowering your shoulder blades". — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/158/lat-pulldown/)
- Torso lean: lean back slightly, **no more than about 30 degrees**. Head aligned with the spine. **Do not increase the backward lean during the pull.** — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/158/lat-pulldown/)
- Path and end point: pull the bar toward the upper or mid chest, driving the elbows down and toward the sides of the torso. Stop when the bar nears or touches the chest, **or when the elbows stop moving downward and start moving backward**. Pulling further adds stress to the shoulder joint. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/158/lat-pulldown/)
- Return: pause briefly, then let the bar rise slowly until the elbows are fully extended, allowing the shoulder blades to rise slightly at the top. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/158/lat-pulldown/)
- Grip width: Andersen et al. 2014 had 15 men do 6RM pulldowns with pronated grips at 1, 1.5 and 2 times biacromial distance. The narrow and medium grips allowed slightly more load than the wide grip (about 80 vs 77 kg). Lat, trapezius and infraspinatus activation was similar over the whole movement, and the wide grip showed more lat activity only in the eccentric phase. The authors suggest a medium grip may have minor advantages, but that anything between 1 and 2 biacromial widths gives similar activation. (Abstract details as reported in search results. PubMed page not fetchable.) — [Andersen et al. 2014, PubMed 24662157](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24662157/); summary at [Sci-Sport](https://sci-sport.com/en/does-grip-width-influence-muscle-activity-during-lat-pull-down-106/)
- Front vs behind the neck, EMG: Sperandei et al. 2009 had 24 trained men do behind-neck, front and V-bar pulldowns at 80% 1RM. Secondary sources disagree on whether lat activity differed (one says no difference, another says the front was higher). Both say the behind-neck version raised posterior deltoid and biceps activity, and that the authors favoured the front pulldown. Original not checked. — [Sperandei et al. 2009, PubMed 19855311](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19855311/); secondary: [Bodybuilding.com](https://bodybuilding.com/fun/front-vs-rear-pull-downs-which-is-better.html)
- Signorile et al. 2002 compared four grips (wide front, wide behind, close, supinated) in about 10 subjects. Secondary sources say wide-grip **anterior** pulldowns gave the highest lat EMG in both phases. Original not checked. — [Western Sydney University record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/electromyographic-analysis-of-three-different-types-of-tat-pull-d/)
- Shoulder risk of behind-neck: a document citing Buchberger describes the behind-head pulldown as placing the shoulder in 90 degrees abduction with 90 degrees external rotation, which can contribute to instability and impingement. — [OnCourt PDF](https://oncourt.ca/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/Overhand-sport-and-Weight-training.pdf). Kolber is reported, via a magazine profile, as finding a strong link between shoulder pain and moves done in this "high-five" position, behind-neck pulldown included. Secondhand. — [Men's Health Australia](https://menshealth.com.au/how-to-shore-up-your-shoulders/). Kolber's 2010 JSCR review reports that up to 36% of resistance-training injuries are at the shoulder (as summarised in search results; the PDF text was not readable). — [Kolber et al. 2010, PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Shoulder%20Injuries%20Attributed%20to%20Resistance%20Training%20-%20A%20Brief%20Review.pdf)
- No prospective injury study comparing front and behind-neck pulldowns was found. The case against behind-neck is EMG (no benefit) plus a biomechanical position argument. — [search synthesis; e.g. Bodybuilding.com](https://bodybuilding.com/fun/front-vs-rear-pull-downs-which-is-better.html)

### Inferences
- **Camera:** a pure side view (sagittal) shows everything that matters: the lean, the bar path in front of the face, the bar reaching the upper chest, and the elbows travelling down beside the ribs. Its weakness is that grip width is invisible. A **3/4 front-side view (about 30 to 45 degrees off the side) [illustration default]** shows grip width and the "elbows down to the sides" path, and the bar can still be seen passing in front of the face. Recommend 3/4 if the style allows, otherwise side.
- **Machine:** a tall upright frame with a high pulley over the seat. A cable drops vertically to a long bar, slightly bent down at the ends. A padded seat with a horizontal thigh pad (roller) above the knees. Feet flat on the floor, knees about 90 degrees **[illustration default]**. The weight stack sits behind or in front of the user and rises as the bar comes down. Showing a few plates rising and falling is a cheap, honest motion cue.
- **Grip:** overhand (pronated), thumbs wrapped, hands about 1.5 times shoulder width **[illustration default; inside Andersen's 1 to 2 biacromial range]**. Wrists straight, knuckles up.
- **Start = end pose:** arms fully extended overhead (elbows straight but not shoulders shrugged to the ears), bar above the head. Torso leaning back about 15 to 20 degrees **[illustration default, under ACE's 30-degree cap]**. Thighs under the pad, chest up, head neutral.
- **Turnaround:** bar at the top of the chest (just under the collarbones), elbows pointing down and slightly back beside the ribs, forearms roughly vertical from the side, chest up. **Torso angle identical to the start.** Shoulder blades pulled down and together.
- **Path:** the shoulder blades drop first (a small visible lowering of the shoulders while the arms are still straight), then the elbows drive down. Bar path: vertical, just in front of the face. Return slower than the pull, e.g. about 2 s down and 2.5 s up plus pauses **[illustration default]**.
- **Faults never to show:** (1) the bar pulled **behind the neck** with the head pushed forward; (2) **swinging the torso back** during the pull or a big lean (over 30 degrees) to cheat the weight; (3) the hips lifting off the seat, or the thighs coming out from under the pad, on the return, and also the shoulders shrugging to the ears with the bar yanked up fast.

### Gaps
- The exact torso-lean angle in the studies (ACE gives only a cap of about 30 degrees). The 15 to 20 degree default is mine.
- Full text of Sperandei 2009 and Andersen 2014 not read (PubMed blocked automated access). Secondary sources disagree on Sperandei's lat result.
- No NSCA, ACSM or ExRx wording obtained.

## Triceps pushdown (cable, straight or short bar, standing)

### Takeaway
ACE: stand facing the stack, feet hip-width or staggered. Full grip with thumbs wrapped. Torso **vertical** (ACE) and shoulders back and down. Upper arms firmly at the sides with the elbows at the midline of the trunk. Wrists neutral. Only the forearms move, to full elbow extension without a hard lockout, and back up until the elbows would start to drift forward, which is about upper-chest height (ACE), or "forearms just past parallel" (NFPT). The two sources disagree on lean: ACE says a vertical torso, while many guides allow a slight forward lean. Use upright to very slight.

### Cited Findings
- Setup: stand facing the cable machine, attachment set above head height, feet hip-width apart or in a staggered stance. Grip the handle or rope firmly with thumbs wrapped around it. — [ACE Triceps Pushdowns](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/lifestyle/exercise-library/185/triceps-pushdowns)
- Torso: brace the abdominals, keep the natural arch in the low back, keep the **torso vertical** and hold that throughout. Shoulders pulled back and down for the whole set. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/lifestyle/exercise-library/185/triceps-pushdowns)
- Start: press the handle down until the elbows are at the midpoint of the trunk with the upper arms firmly at the sides. Wrists neutral, in line with the forearms. ACE gives no forearm angle and no distance from the machine. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/lifestyle/exercise-library/185/triceps-pushdowns)
- Push: exhale and straighten the elbows, pressing down toward the floor, keeping torso, shoulders, upper arms and wrists still. End when the elbows are fully extended, **without "locking out"**. Brief pause. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/lifestyle/exercise-library/185/triceps-pushdowns)
- Return: slowly let the elbows bend. **Stop when the elbows begin to drift forward from the trunk's midline, around upper-chest height.** Elbows drifting forward shifts work to the lats. — [ACE](https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/lifestyle/exercise-library/185/triceps-pushdowns)
- NFPT (straight bar): elbows tucked at the sides throughout. Wrists straight. Medium grip has the hands directly in front of the shoulders. Raise the bar over 2 to 3 seconds until the **forearms are just past parallel to the floor**. Raising higher shifts work to the forearms. Do not lock the elbows ("can result in injury to your elbows"). Moving faster than 2 to 3 seconds reduces control. Elbows drifting out bring in the lats and shoulders. — [NFPT straight-bar pushdown](https://nfpt.com/how-to-do-straight-bar-tricep-pushdown/)
- Leaning far over the bar and flaring the elbows turns it into something closer to a dip. Keep the chest up and elbows tucked. (Commercial guide, as summarised in search results.) — [FitnessVolt](https://fitnessvolt.com/?p=12518)
- No university, physiotherapy or NSCA source was found for this exercise. ACE is the best authority reached.

### Inferences
- **Camera:** **pure side view** is best. The single moving joint (elbow) is in the sagittal plane, so a side view shows the pinned upper arm, the forearm arc and the cable line at once. A 3/4 view foreshortens the forearm arc. Place the camera on the side away from the stack's frame, or show the stack as a simple column so it does not hide the arm.
- **Machine:** a tall cable column with a high pulley above head height. The cable runs from the pulley down and slightly toward the user to the middle of a short straight bar (about shoulder width, 50 to 60 cm **[illustration default]**). Weight plates in the column rise as the bar is pushed down.
- **Stance:** about one short step (about 30 cm **[illustration default]**) from the column, so the cable hangs nearly vertical in front of the chest and the bar clears the body. Feet hip-width. Knees soft. Torso upright or leaning forward at most about 10 degrees **[illustration default; ACE says vertical]**.
- **Grip:** overhand (pronated), thumbs wrapped, hands about shoulder width (NFPT "directly in front of the shoulders"). Wrists straight, knuckles forward/up, no wrist bending.
- **Start = end pose:** elbows bent with the forearms slightly above horizontal (about 80 to 90 degrees of elbow flexion **[illustration default]**, consistent with NFPT "just past parallel" and ACE "upper-chest height"). Bar at lower-to-mid chest height a hand's width in front of the body. Upper arms vertical along the ribs.
- **Turnaround:** elbows straight but soft (not snapped back), bar at the front of the upper thighs. Upper arms **in exactly the same place** as at the start.
- **Path:** the hands travel on an arc centred on the elbow. Nothing else moves: no shoulder motion, no torso bob. Roughly 2 s down, 0.3 s pause, 2.5 s up **[illustration default]**.
- **Faults never to show:** (1) **elbows drifting forward or flaring out** as the bar rises (the upper arm swinging, which turns it into a lat/press movement); (2) **leaning the bodyweight over the bar / torso pumping** to push it down; (3) bent or cocked wrists, and a hard snap into elbow lockout at the bottom.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or institutional source for the exact top-of-range forearm angle. ACE ("upper-chest height") and NFPT ("just past parallel") broadly agree.
- No sourced distance from the machine or forward-lean angle. ACE explicitly says vertical, so any lean is a stylistic default.

## Leg press (45-degree plate-loaded sled)

### Takeaway
Sources agree. Back and hips stay against the pad. Feet flat, about hip- to shoulder-width, mid-platform. Lower under control to about 90 degrees at the knee, or less if the hips begin to roll off the seat, since that rounding loads the lumbar spine. Press back up without locking the knees. Knees track in line with the toes and do not cave in. Release the safety catches at the start and use the side handles. Escamilla et al. found knee forces rise with knee flexion, which supports stopping around 90 degrees for a general audience.

### Cited Findings
- Setup (NFPT): sit with the back against the seat and feet on the platform. Press the platform out until the legs are almost straight, without locking the knees. **Flip out the outside handles** (the safety stops) and hold the inside handles. — [NFPT leg press](https://www.nfpt.com/blog/how-to-do-leg-presses)
- Feet (NFPT): medium stance is about shoulder-width and works the whole quad, hamstrings and glutes. The lower part of the platform is used for even emphasis. — [NFPT](https://www.nfpt.com/blog/how-to-do-leg-presses). Hawaii Pacific Health (Wellness Network/WebMD Ignite): feet flat on the pad with equal distance between ankles, knees and hips. — [Hawaii Pacific Health](https://livinghealthy.hawaiipacifichealth.org/wellness/fitness/your-exercise-routine-leg-press)
- Depth: start with about a **90-degree bend at the knee**. — [Hawaii Pacific Health](https://livinghealthy.hawaiipacifichealth.org/wellness/fitness/your-exercise-routine-leg-press). NFPT: lower over 2 to 3 seconds until the shin is parallel to the floor or just past. — [NFPT](https://www.nfpt.com/blog/how-to-do-leg-presses)
- Lockout: do not lock the knees at the top. Keep a small bend. — [Hawaii Pacific Health](https://livinghealthy.hawaiipacifichealth.org/wellness/fitness/your-exercise-routine-leg-press); [NFPT](https://www.nfpt.com/blog/how-to-do-leg-presses)
- Hips and back: hips or buttocks lifting off the seat increases lower-back stress. Lowering so far that the hips rise can cause serious lower-back injury. Brace the abdominals throughout. — [NFPT](https://www.nfpt.com/blog/how-to-do-leg-presses). Do not strain or hyperextend the back to push. — [Hawaii Pacific Health](https://livinghealthy.hawaiipacifichealth.org/wellness/fitness/your-exercise-routine-leg-press)
- A university human-movement text (eCampusOntario Pressbooks) notes that lifting the hips off the seat pad can increase stress on the lumbar spine (as quoted in search results; the page returned 403 to direct fetch). — [eCampusOntario, Human Movement](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/humanmovement/?p=1351)
- Excessive knee flexion combined with posterior pelvic tilt in a deep leg press can increase shear on the lumbar spine and posterior knee structures (claim in a registered clinical-trial protocol, attributed to Escamilla 2001). — [ClinicalTrials.gov protocol NCT07351097](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/97/NCT07351097/Prot_SAP_ICF_000.pdf)
- Escamilla et al. 2001 (Med Sci Sports Exerc 33(9):1552-66): knee compressive forces were generally higher in the squat than in high- or low-foot leg press. All measured knee forces **rose with knee flexion**. 0 to 50 degrees of flexion may suit people trying to minimise knee forces. No ACL forces in any variation. (Abstract as reported in search results.) — [Escamilla et al. 2001, PubMed 11528346](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11528346/)
- Knee cave increases injury risk. The usual cue is knees in line with the toes. Higher foot placement involves the glutes more but makes the hips more likely to round off. (Trainer and fitness guides, as summarised in search results.) — [Livestrong, leg press mistakes](https://livestrong.com/article/13773374-leg-press-mistakes); [Gravitus guide](https://gravitus.com/guides/exercises/leg-press)

### Inferences
- **Camera:** **pure side view**, from the side without the plate horns if possible. The sled rail at 45 degrees, the knee angle, the shin and the hip-to-pad contact are all sagittal, and the side view is the only one that shows the pelvis staying on the pad (the key safety point). A 3/4 view would show knee-over-toe tracking but hide the butt wink. Side view wins.
- **Machine:** a low reclined seat. The backrest is reclined well back (about 30 to 45 degrees from horizontal **[illustration default; varies by brand, no source]**), with a seat pan under the hips. Two parallel rails rise at 45 degrees away from the seat. On them sits a sled with a large flat footplate facing the user, with plate horns on its sides loaded with a few plates. Short handles beside the seat for the hands, plus safety-stop levers at the sled sides (released at the start: the art can show the levers simply flipped open and not touched in the loop).
- **Body:** head, upper back, lower back and buttocks all on the pad. Hands on the side handles. Feet flat on the middle of the plate, about hip- to shoulder-width, toes slightly out (about 10 to 15 degrees **[illustration default]**).
- **Start = end pose:** sled up, legs extended with a **visible small bend** at the knees (about 10 to 15 degrees of flexion **[illustration default]**). Not locked.
- **Turnaround:** sled lowered along the rail until the knees are about 90 degrees (Hawaii Pacific Health; NFPT's "shin parallel to the floor" yields a similar picture on a 45-degree machine). Thighs moving toward the chest but **not** squashing the belly. **Buttocks and lower back still flat on the pad**, heels still down. From the side the knee sits roughly above the toes.
- **Path:** the sled slides in a straight line along the rail. Feet stay glued to the plate. Only the hips, knees and ankles fold. About 2.3 s down, 0.2 s pause, 2 s up **[illustration default]**. The plates move with the sled, which is the main moving mass in the frame.
- **Faults never to show:** (1) **hips curling off the seat / lower back rounding** at the bottom (the depth that causes it is too deep); (2) **knees locked straight** (or snapped back) at the top; (3) **knees caving inward**, or heels lifting off the plate so the press is on the toes. Also never show hands pushing on the knees.

### Gaps
- No NSCA, ACSM or ACE text for the 45-degree leg press was reached.
- No sourced value for the backrest angle of a typical 45-degree sled (brand-dependent). Mine is an illustration default.
- No primary study measuring lumbar flexion against leg-press depth was found. The lumbar argument rests on trainer and university-text statements plus the general flexion-under-load mechanism.
- Escamilla 2001's full text, including which leg-press machine was used (45-degree sled or horizontal), was not checked.
